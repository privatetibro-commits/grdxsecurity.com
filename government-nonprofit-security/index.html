Short answer

GRDX provides security officers for government buildings, community centers and shelters in Northern Virginia, D.C. and Maryland. Officers screen entrances, manage tense public interactions, support staff at service counters and cover overnight shelter shifts, all licensed, uniformed and backed by a supervisor reachable 24/7.

What security does a public building need?

Most public buildings need a controlled front entrance, someone near the service counters, and a plan for after-hours meetings.

Permit offices, courts annexes, social service sites and town halls all see people on hard days. A resident whose benefits were cut, a contractor fighting a stop-work order, a neighbor at a heated zoning hearing. Officers stay visible, listen, and step in when a conversation starts to slip.

  • Entrance screening where your policy requires it
  • Presence at service counters and waiting areas
  • Coverage for evening board and council meetings
  • Lockup and alarm checks after hours

Community and recreation centers

Community centers mix kids, seniors and the general public in one building, often from early morning into the night.

Youth programs, senior lunches, rentals for birthday parties and late-night basketball each bring a different crowd. Officers help with check-in, watch the parking lot at closing and handle disputes on the court before they spill outside.

Food distribution days deserve their own mention. Lines form early, tempers get short in the heat or cold, and volunteers are not trained to manage a crowd. An officer at the head of the line keeps things orderly and lets volunteers focus on handing out food.

Many centers also rent space to outside groups on weekends. An officer at those events protects the building and gives the renter someone to call if a guest becomes a problem.

Security at homeless and family shelters

Shelter security works when officers treat residents with respect and stay steady through long nights.

Intake hours can be tense. Someone is turned away for a rule violation; an ex-partner shows up at a family shelter; two residents argue over a bunk. Our leadership includes an FBI-certified hostage negotiator, and that influences how officers are briefed to talk first. Officers follow your house rules and your case managers' guidance.

Overnight, the officer also checks exits and fire doors on a set schedule.

Supporting front-line staff

Front counter staff in public buildings take most of the anger, so the officer's first job is to back them up.

We talk with staff before the first shift about which situations wear on them most. Some want an officer standing nearby whenever a certain program has appointments. Others want a simple signal, a word or gesture, that brings the officer over without alarming the room.

For shelters and community centers, we meet with the program director and case managers to agree on what officers handle and what stays with staff. Clear lines prevent the officer from overstepping into casework and keep residents' trust intact.

Reports can be formatted to match what your agency or grant funder asks for, whether that is a daily log, a weekly summary or incident forms filed within a set time.

Oversight, accountability and first contact

Public money calls for clear records. Officers file time-stamped photo reports, supervisors inspect posts, and patrols are GPS-verified. Our compliance lead is a retired sheriff's office Inspector General. Every officer is licensed and verified before scheduling and can be replaced on request.
